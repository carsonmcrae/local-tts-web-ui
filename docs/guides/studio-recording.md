# Recording a studio voice session

A studio voice is built from a longer, carefully recorded session instead of one short clip. The app
measures every take, averages the speaker's identity across the whole session, and builds the voice
from the cleanest, most natural passage. This guide covers recording the session in an audio editor
such as [Audacity](https://www.audacityteam.org/) and importing it; the in-app studio recorder shows
the same passages.

Only record yourself, or someone who has given you explicit permission to create a synthetic copy of
their voice. Recordings stay on this computer.

## Set up (10 minutes)

**Microphone**

- Use a USB or XLR microphone. Laptop and headset microphones are usually too bright ("tinny"), and
  the voice copies that sound.
- Put it 15–20 cm (a hand's width) from your mouth, aimed slightly to the side of your mouth rather
  than straight at it. Use a pop filter if you have one.
- Keep the same distance for the whole session.

**Windows**

- Settings → System → Sound → your microphone → **Audio enhancements: Off**.
- Under Properties, set the format to **48,000 Hz, 24-bit** if it's offered.

**Room**

- Choose a small room with soft furnishings (curtains, a sofa, a rug). Bare walls echo.
- Turn off fans, heaters and air conditioning while you record, and silence your phone.

**Audacity**

- Audio Setup → Host: **Windows WASAPI**; Recording device: your microphone; **1 (Mono)** channel.
- Project Rate (bottom left): **48000 Hz**.
- Set the microphone gain so your loudest words peak around **−12 dB** on the recording meter. Never
  let it reach 0.

## Record (20–30 minutes)

1. Press record and stay silent for **5 seconds** first. The app uses this to measure the room.
2. Read the **foundation passage** first, then the others below. Speak as if you were talking to a
   friend, not reading a list: let your voice rise and fall naturally. Each passage takes about 10–15
   seconds (the foundation passage about a minute), and the note in italics under it says which
   inflection and pauses it teaches. Those differences are what let the voice ask questions, talk in
   dialogue and sound lively the way you do.
3. Pause for **2 seconds** between passages, so the app can split them.
4. If you stumble, pause for 2 seconds and read the whole passage again. Keep going; the app keeps the
   cleanest take.
5. Take breaks whenever you like. Stop recording, rest, and continue in a new recording.

All 48 passages give about 10–12 minutes of speech, which is plenty. Five minutes gives a complete
voice. Short on time? The foundation passage plus 45 seconds of speech in all builds an early version
that you can improve later by recording more.

## Export

File → Export Audio → **WAV (Microsoft), Signed 24-bit PCM** or **FLAC**, mono, 48000 Hz. Then import
it in Voice Studio → Create a voice → Studio voice → Import a recording.

## Passages

### Foundation (record this first)

Good morning, and welcome. Have you ever noticed how a single sound can bring back a whole memory?
For me, it's rain on a tin roof. The moment I hear it, I'm eight years old again, sitting on my
grandmother's porch with a cup of cocoa. "Don't spill it," she'd say, laughing, "or the cat will
want some too!" Those summers felt endless. We picked blackberries until our fingers turned purple,
counted the seconds between lightning and thunder, one, two, three, four, and argued about whether
the storm was coming closer. Some evenings were loud and busy; others were so quiet you could hear
the clock ticking in the kitchen. Now, whenever it rains, I slow down a little. I make something
warm to drink, open the window just a crack, and listen. It's funny, isn't it? The simplest things
stay with us the longest.

_How to read it: Read it in one go, like a story told to a friend (about a minute). Let the opening
question rise, laugh a little in the grandmother's line, count “one, two, three, four” slowly, and
slow down for the calm ending. This passage alone is enough to build a first version of your voice._

### Narration

1. The library opens at nine, but most mornings the first visitors arrive a little early. They wait
   on the stone steps with coffee in hand, talking quietly about the books they plan to find.

   _Steady, even narration. Pause briefly after “nine” and let the last sentence settle down on
   “find”._

2. Rivers shape the land slowly. Over thousands of years, water carves valleys, moves stones, and
   carries soil far downstream, where it builds the wide, flat plains that farmers depend on.

   _Calm and informative. Give each item in “carves valleys, moves stones, and carries soil” a small
   lift, and fall on the last._

3. Before the bridge was built, the only way across was a small ferry. It ran every half hour in
   summer, and only twice a day once the cold weather arrived.

   _An easy storytelling pace. Pause after “built”, and contrast “every half hour” with “only twice
   a day”._

4. Good bread needs very little: flour, water, salt, and time. The dough rests overnight, and by
   morning it has risen, ready to be shaped and baked until the crust turns golden.

   _Pause at the colon, then read “flour, water, salt, and time” as a slow list that falls on
   “time”._

5. The museum's newest exhibit traces the history of maps, from rough sketches on clay tablets to
   the detailed satellite images we now carry in our pockets every day.

   _One long, flowing sentence: keep it moving, take a small breath after “maps”, and fall gently at
   the end._

6. In early spring, the garden looks bare. Then, almost overnight, small green shoots push through
   the soil, and within a few weeks the beds are full of colour.

   _Start a little flat on “the garden looks bare”, then brighten on “Then, almost overnight”: a
   change of mood._

7. Most of the town's old houses were built from local timber. Their wide porches face the street, a
   reminder of a time when neighbours spent long evenings outside together.

   _Warm and unhurried. Lean slightly on “a reminder of a time” and let the last words trail off
   softly._

8. The train left the station on time, winding through the hills as the sun came up. By noon, the
   forests had given way to open fields and distant mountains.

   _An even travelling pace, with a clear pause after “By noon”._

### Storytelling

9. She opened the letter slowly, almost afraid to read it. Then her face changed completely. "I got
   the job," she whispered, and then, much louder, "I actually got the job!"

   _Build suspense: slow and hesitant at first, say “I got the job” softly (not a full whisper), then let the last line
   burst out, loud and joyful._

10. The storm hit just after midnight. Thunder shook the windows, the lights flickered once, twice,
    and then the whole house went dark. For a moment, nobody said a word.

    _Dramatic: speed up through “once, twice, and then”, pause before “the whole house went dark”,
    and say the last sentence quietly._

11. Grandpa leaned back in his chair and smiled. "Let me tell you about the summer I tried to build
    a boat," he said. "It floated for exactly three minutes."

    _Two voices: your own for the narration, a warm, older voice for Grandpa. Smile on “exactly
    three minutes”._

12. We had been walking for hours when we finally reached the top. The wind was cold and our legs
    were tired, but the view stretched for miles, and it was worth every single step.

    _Sound tired at the start, then open up with relief on “the view stretched for miles”. Stress
    “every single step”._

13. The dog heard the car before anyone else. He raced to the door, tail wagging wildly, and when it
    finally opened, he jumped up as if he hadn't seen us in years.

    _Lively and fond. Quicken on “tail wagging wildly” and land the ending with a smile._

14. Nobody expected the old piano to still work. But when Maya pressed the first key, a clear, warm
    note filled the room, and everyone turned to listen.

    _Start doubtful and quiet, soften with wonder on “a clear, warm note”, and slow down for the
    last words._

15. I was sure I'd locked the door. I checked my pockets, my bag, the car. Nothing. And then I saw
    the keys, hanging right there in the lock, exactly where I had left them.

    _Rising frustration: short, clipped words for “my pockets, my bag, the car. Nothing.” Then a
    sheepish, slower ending._

16. The crowd went silent as the runner rounded the final corner. She was falling behind, but
    somehow, in the last few metres, she found one more burst of speed and crossed the line first.

    _Hushed tension at first, getting faster and louder towards “crossed the line first”._

### Conversation

17. Honestly, I don't mind driving. It's a nice change, and if we leave around eight, we'll miss
    most of the traffic. You can pick the music, though. That's only fair.

    _Relaxed and friendly, like talking to a passenger. A light, playful tone on “That's only
    fair.”_

18. Oh, that reminds me, did you ever hear back about the apartment? I know you were really hoping
    for the one near the park. Fingers crossed, right?

    _Casual, remembering something mid-thought on “Oh, that reminds me”. Let “right?” rise at the
    end._

19. So here's the plan. I'll grab the groceries on my way home, you start the pasta, and we'll eat
    around seven. Sound good? Great. See you then.

    _Brisk and practical. “Sound good?” rises, “Great.” falls, and a short pause comes before “See
    you then.”_

20. I tried that new place downtown last night. The food was fine, nothing special, but the dessert?
    Wow. I'd go back just for that chocolate cake.

    _Unimpressed at first (“nothing special”), then a real pause before “Wow.” and genuine delight._

21. No, no, it's totally okay. These things happen. Let's just move it to next week. Tuesday works
    for me, or Thursday afternoon, whatever's easier for you.

    _Reassuring and kind. Say “No, no” softly, and give “Tuesday” and “Thursday afternoon” each a
    small lift._

22. You know what's funny? I spent all weekend looking for my glasses, and they were on my head the
    entire time. I'm not even a little embarrassed. Okay, maybe a little.

    _Amused: let a smile come through, and pause briefly before “Okay, maybe a little.”_

23. Thanks so much for helping with the move. I really couldn't have done it without you. Next time
    you need anything, and I mean anything, just let me know.

    _Sincere thanks. Stress “really” and “anything”, with small pauses around “and I mean
    anything”._

24. Well, I think it went pretty well, all things considered. A couple of questions caught me off
    guard, but I stayed calm, and they said they'd call by Friday.

    _Thoughtful and a little relieved: softer on “all things considered”, confident at the end._

### Questions and surprise

25. Wait, you're telling me the whole thing was planned from the start? The surprise party, the fake
    meeting, all of it? I can't believe nobody said a thing!

    _Growing disbelief: each question rises higher than the last, and the final line is an
    exclamation._

26. Have you ever wondered why the sky turns orange at sunset? Or why the ocean looks blue on some
    days and grey on others? The answers are surprisingly simple.

    _Curious questions that rise at the end, then a last sentence that falls with a knowing tone._

27. What would you do with an entire free day? Would you sleep in, go for a long walk, call an old
    friend, or finally finish that book on your nightstand?

    _A dreamy, open question. Let each option in the list rise a little and the last one fall
    gently._

28. Really? Tomorrow? That's amazing news! Does anyone else know yet, or am I the first? Oh, we
    absolutely have to celebrate this weekend.

    _Pure excitement: short questions with a big rise (“Really? Tomorrow?”), then a bright,
    celebrating ending._

29. Is it just me, or is it getting colder in here? Did someone leave the window open again? I'm
    sure it was closed when I left this morning.

    _Puzzled and a little annoyed: the questions rise, and the last sentence is flat and certain._

30. Can you believe how fast this year went by? It feels like we were just planning the summer trip,
    and now the holidays are almost here.

    _Surprised and wistful on the opening question, then slow and reflective._

31. Which way is the station from here? Left at the lights, or straight past the church? I'm
    completely turned around, and my phone just died.

    _Lost and slightly stressed: quick questions, and a sigh in your voice on “and my phone just
    died”._

32. Oh no, I completely forgot! The meeting was today? At three? I'm so sorry. Is there any way we
    could still catch the end of it?

    _Panic, then apology: fast and loud on “I completely forgot!”, softer and sincere on “I'm so
    sorry”._

### Numbers, names and lists

33. Your order number is four, seven, two, nine, one. It should arrive on Thursday, the fourteenth
    of March, between ten in the morning and two in the afternoon.

    _Read the order number digit by digit with short, even pauses, then slow and clear for the date
    and times._

34. For the recipe, you'll need two cups of flour, one teaspoon of baking soda, half a cup of sugar,
    three eggs, and a pinch of salt.

    _A recipe list: pause briefly after each ingredient, and fall on “a pinch of salt”._

35. The meeting has moved to room three-oh-five on the third floor. Please bring your laptop, the
    quarterly report, and any questions you'd like to raise.

    _Clear and businesslike: say “three-oh-five” crisply, then a steady list._

36. Flight two-eighteen to Vancouver is now boarding at gate twenty-six. Passengers in rows twenty
    through thirty-five, please make your way to the gate.

    _Like an announcement: clear and a little formal, with pauses after the flight number and the
    gate._

37. The museum is open Tuesday through Sunday, from nine thirty until five. Tickets cost fifteen
    dollars for adults and eight for students, and children under six get in free.

    _Informative and friendly. Keep the prices clearly apart and smile on “get in free”._

38. Our team this season includes Priya, Marcus, Elena, Jonah, and Wei. Together, they scored
    forty-three goals in twelve games, a new club record.

    _Say each name distinctly with a small pause between them, and sound proud on “a new club
    record”._

39. In nineteen sixty-nine, hundreds of millions of people watched as astronauts stepped onto the
    surface of the moon, almost four hundred thousand kilometres away.

    _Documentary narration: steady and in awe, with weight on “four hundred thousand kilometres”._

40. To reset the device, hold the power button for ten seconds, wait until the light blinks twice,
    then press it once more. The whole process takes about a minute.

    _Step-by-step instructions: calm, with a clear pause between the steps._

### Calm and reflective

41. There's something peaceful about early mornings. The house is quiet, the light is soft, and for
    a little while, it feels like the whole day is still waiting to begin.

    _Soft and slow. Let your voice relax and leave space after each comma._

42. Take a slow breath in, and let it out gently. Notice the weight of your hands, the sounds around
    you, and the steady rhythm of your own breathing.

    _Very calm, like a guided meditation. Breathe where the sentence breathes, and lower your voice
    slightly._

43. Some of the best conversations happen late at night, when there's nowhere to be and no reason to
    hurry. Words come slowly, and people say what they really mean.

    _Quiet and close, as if late at night. Slow down on “say what they really mean”._

44. I used to think happiness was something you found. Now I think it's something you notice, in
    small moments, like warm sunlight on a cold afternoon.

    _A reflective contrast: “found” and “notice” carry the meaning, so give them gentle weight._

45. The lake was perfectly still that evening. We sat on the dock without talking, watching the
    colours fade from the sky, until the first stars appeared.

    _A hushed evening tone, slow and even, with a soft fall on “until the first stars appeared”._

46. Learning anything new takes patience. At first, every step feels clumsy and slow. But little by
    little, what once seemed impossible becomes something you simply do.

    _Encouraging and patient, a little brighter on “becomes something you simply do”._

47. When the snow falls at night, the whole town seems to hold its breath. The streets go quiet, the
    lamps glow softly, and everything feels a little more gentle.

    _Gentle and hushed, soft but still voiced on “hold its breath”, and warm on the last line._

48. Thank you for listening. Whatever today brings, I hope it holds a few quiet moments, a good
    conversation, and at least one reason to smile.

    _A warm sign-off: smile while you read, and let the final words land softly._
